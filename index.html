Environmental stressors and financial market risk

My thesis examined how wildfire smoke, PM2.5, and temperature relate to the returns and volatility of major US stock indices from 2010 to 2024 across the eight largest US cities. I combined NOAA Hazard Mapping System smoke products and EPA air-quality data with financial data, running OLS regressions with economic and weather controls over 22 smoke-variable model combinations and 44 indices, and Random Forest and XGBoost models to capture nonlinear effects. High-smoke days were associated with higher returns and lower volatility in some large cities, while temperature showed the strongest relationship, though results were not consistent across models.

Thesis

Environmental Stressors and Financial Market Risk: Evidence from Wildfire Smoke and Temperature Across US Cities

Karthick Mohan Kumar, Shantanu Jathar, Christian L'Orange, Jesse Burkhardt, Ellison Carter

MS thesis · Colorado State University

Abstract

Wildfire smoke increasingly contributes to poor air quality in the United States and adversely affects public health, visibility, and ecosystems. This study examines how wildfire smoke, PM2.5, and temperature affect the returns and volatility of major US stock indices, using NOAA Hazard Mapping System smoke products, EPA Air Quality System PM2.5 data, and financial data from 2010 to 2024 across the eight largest US cities. OLS regressions with economic and weather controls were run over 22 smoke-variable model combinations and 44 indices, complemented by Random Forest and XGBoost models to capture nonlinear relationships. On high-smoke days, New York City and some other large cities saw higher returns and lower volatility, while temperature showed the strongest relationship among weather and air-quality variables. Results are not consistent across models, suggesting coincidence rather than a causal relationship.
